# Formal verification

Proving programs correct with mathematics instead of (only) testing — anchored by [Leonardo de Moura](../entities/leonardo-de-moura.md), who built the Lean proof assistant most of this work now runs on, [Xavier Leroy](../entities/xavier-leroy.md), who spent years proving the CompCert C compiler correct, and [Leslie Lamport](../entities/leslie-lamport.md), for whom proof simply *is* understanding. All three build on [Dijkstra](../entities/edsger-dijkstra.md)'s aphorism, which both Leroy and de Moura quote in their opening minutes: "testing can only show the presence of bugs, but not their complete absence" ([Leroy](../sources/creator-of-ocaml-functional-programming.md); [de Moura](../sources/creator-of-lean-the-end-of-handwritten.md)).

## What the guests say

### What it is and what it has actually achieved

Leroy gives the podcast's most concrete tour ([episode](../sources/creator-of-ocaml-functional-programming.md)): specifications range from "never crashes on well-formed inputs / all array accesses in bounds" (simple, yet beyond what type systems or testing ensure — crashing code is attackable code) up to termination, non-leakage of confidential data, and precise floating-point error bounds. Proof assistants (Lean, Coq/Rocq, Isabelle) grew out of computer-assisted mathematics, not programming: automatic theorem proving proved too hard, so they became formal languages where the machine does small steps, the human guides big ones, and the finished proof is machine-recheckable — no forgotten cases, no circular hypotheses. Landmark results: CompCert (the emitted assembly is proved faithful to the C source — a proof that would run "several thousand pages" on paper) and Australia's seL4 microkernel, 8,000 lines of "extremely technical" C, every line proved. Mechanization is also what makes verified software *evolvable*: adapt the proofs and be sure you introduced no regression. On the halting-problem objection: undecidability only forbids a universal analyzer — "you only need to be able to do it for the programs you really care about," and CompCert's own termination is among its proved properties.

De Moura supplies the tool-builder's version of the same picture ([episode](../sources/creator-of-lean-the-end-of-handwritten.md)). Lean is a programming language in which you can also state and prove properties; code written in *other* languages is reached two ways — *shallow embedding*, where a tool like Aeneas maps Rust into Lean and you verify the translation, and *deep embedding*, where you write the semantics of C inside Lean so that C programs become Lean objects you can state theorems about. His compression of the whole discipline: "if you can express in math what you care about in your program, you can verify using Lean." The trusted base is deliberately tiny and plural: proof checking *is* type checking, a high-performance kernel is about 5,000 lines — "the goal of the kernel should be something you can write yourself" — and independent kernels exist in Lean (Mario Carneiro's Lean-for-Lean, itself verified against Lean's semantics) and in Rust, with pretty-printers so you can confirm that the theorem exported really was Fermat's Last Theorem "and not 2 plus 3 equals 4." Lean itself is unverified and, he argues, unverifiable in principle: nobody can pin a stable specification on something like the simplifier while users keep asking to change its behaviour.

The scaling profiles of the two application domains are opposite, which is the field's live engineering problem: in math the statements are tiny (Fermat's Last Theorem) and the proofs deep; in software the statements are big and the proofs shallow but forced to manipulate enormous objects ([de Moura](../sources/creator-of-lean-the-end-of-handwritten.md)).

### Automatic vs interactive: why Lean exists at all

De Moura built both sides of this and is the corpus's only guest who can compare them from the inside ([episode](../sources/creator-of-lean-the-end-of-handwritten.md)). Z3, which he started at Microsoft Research around 2006, is a fully automatic SMT solver: push-button constraint solving over background theories (arithmetic, arrays) picked for test-case generation and program analysis. It is superb at *finding* bugs — encode a suspicious execution path as constraints and get either "unsatisfiable," meaning the path is unreachable, or a concrete input that triggers it — and at bounded hardware verification. It was "never super successful" at proving the absence of bugs, because once you quantify over program states the problem is undecidable, the heuristics were hand-coded, and they failed exactly where users already knew the answer: "come on, I know the proof. I mean, why can't Z3 find it?" A colleague at Amazon named the resulting fragility *proof instability* — swap the operands of a conjunction and a working proof starts failing. "Lean was born to fill this gap": in an interactive assistant you can supply the reasoning step by step, and — the punchline he did not anticipate when he designed it — so can an AI.

His explanation for why undecidable machinery works in practice at all is one of the better lines in the corpus: "programs and hardware, they are not correct for esoteric reasons... they are correct for very simple reasons" ([complexity-theory](complexity-theory.md)). That is the applied echo of [Avi Wigderson](../entities/avi-wigderson.md)'s point that worst-case hardness says little about practice and structured instances are why SAT solvers work ([episode](../sources/turing-award-winner-p-vs-np-zero.md)).

### Proof as a way of thinking, not just a tool

Lamport arrives at the same place from distributed systems ([episode](../sources/turing-award-winner-on-working-with.md)): "For me, understanding means you can write a proof of it. What understanding means for most people is a warm fuzzy feeling" — the Raft version students found "more understandable" contained a later-discovered bug, and his Bakery algorithm exists because his first "obviously correct" submission came back with a bug. His method: invariants, not behaviors (behavioral reasoning explodes exponentially; invariance proofs grow ~quadratically). De Moura reports Lamport's claim as an observed effect rather than a philosophy — "anybody that went through the process of formalizing something understands the subject way better after that," one level beyond the familiar "I understood the algorithm once I implemented it" ([episode](../sources/creator-of-lean-the-end-of-handwritten.md)). He also has the corpus's most striking counterexample to the idea that proof requires comprehension: in the Liquid Tensor Experiment, Johan Commelin's team formalized *and simplified* an unpublished result of Fields Medalist Peter Scholze without fully understanding it, guided move by move by Lean's Infoview — "how can you simplify a proof from one of the greatest living mathematicians without fully understanding it?" He likens it to refactoring code by gut feel.

Leroy's structural version of the same insight: purely functional style shrinks the gap between program and mathematics, which is why CompCert was written functionally and why formal-methods people dislike assignment ([functional-programming](functional-programming.md)) ([episode](../sources/creator-of-ocaml-functional-programming.md)). Wigderson supplies the theory outer frame: NP is exactly the class of statements whose proofs can be efficiently *checked*, and zero-knowledge shows anything provable can be proved while revealing nothing ([episode](../sources/turing-award-winner-p-vs-np-zero.md)).

### The soft frontier: types as verification-lite

Static type systems are the mass-market end of the same spectrum: [Simon Peyton Jones](../entities/simon-peyton-jones.md) calls GHC's typed Core the reason optimizer bugs get caught at compile time rather than as distant segfaults ([episode](../sources/co-creator-of-haskell-functional.md)), and Leroy notes array-bounds correctness is precisely where types stop and real verification begins ([episode](../sources/creator-of-ocaml-functional-programming.md)). De Moura's dependent types are the far end of that same line, where the distinction collapses entirely: a structure field whose *type* is a proof that x > y, or a function that cannot be called without evidence that y ≠ 0, gives you "invariants for free — you don't have to invent invariants." He chose dependent type theory over the much easier-to-implement higher-order logic on Jeremy Avigad's advice that serious mathematicians would never accept the latter, since abstract math needs structures as first-class citizens (a function taking a group and returning a group) and higher-order logic forces "encoding tricks. It's a mess" ([episode](../sources/creator-of-lean-the-end-of-handwritten.md), [programming-languages](programming-languages.md)).

### Verification meets AI — the live debate, and a 2026-08-10 update

Leroy (2026-07-20) framed generative AI as both the crisis and the opportunity ([episode](../sources/creator-of-ocaml-functional-programming.md)): LLMs already produce plausible Lean proofs that machines can recheck (with caveats — models sometimes quietly weaken the statement or botch auto-formalization), and the same could work for programs: AI ships code *plus* a machine-checkable proof against a spec. But he argued this relocates the hard problem to **specification**: programmers are bad at writing consistent specs, and an unsatisfiable precondition makes any function body "verified" vacuously. His outlook: "maybe this will be the decade of formal verification of software. We've been waiting for that for 50 years."

**De Moura, three weeks later, agrees with the conclusion and disputes the diagnosis.** Asked directly about the decade-of-verification claim he answers "yeah, for sure" — but his account of where the cost actually sat is different, and it comes from running the toolchain rather than from using it ([episode](../sources/creator-of-lean-the-end-of-handwritten.md)):

- The spec is not the painful part. "Properties are usually vaguely in your mind," and his portable trick is that **"an inefficient program can be viewed as a specification"** — write the naive version, then ask the model for the fast one plus a proof of equivalence. Where Leroy sees programmers who can't write specs, de Moura sees programmers who already have properties in mind and lacked a cheap way to discharge them.
- The painful part was **proof maintenance**. Verification historically cost ~10x the program, and the recurring tax was repairing proofs after every code change, often without remembering why they were written — "imagine with proofs, it's the same process" as patching a broken test suite. AI is "extremely good at proving, writing formal proofs, maintaining formal proofs"; he describes asking a model to rewrite proofs *he himself did not understand* so he could remove a language feature.
- The existence proof: Kim Morrison prompted an AI to translate the zlib C compression library into Lean, pass zlib's own test suites, and prove that compressing then decompressing returns the original data. About a week. "It feels like science fiction. Six months ago, I would say this is science fiction."
- The economic before/after, from inside [AWS](../entities/aws.md): "we have been using formal verification for decades, but only for the super-safe, critical components because it's expensive. Until now, right? With AI, it changes the game."
- The adoption argument he actually leads with is not safety but **optimization courage**: engineers refuse to optimize code they don't fully understand, and a proof removes the fear — "if the AI is telling you, look, I optimized it, it still computes the same thing. Here's the proof. Game changer." His own practice: "I'm not writing unit tests anymore. I'm writing properties and proving them. The AI is proving most of them."

He is also blunt about the ceiling: AI finds proofs, not mathematics. "We see no evidence it can find novel proofs" of the concept-inventing kind, and the million-line refutation of the unit distance conjecture is a formal proof, not a new theory — though "I would not bet against AI here."

[Bjarne Stroustrup](../entities/bjarne-stroustrup.md) frames the validation burden from the regulator's side — safety-critical industries must validate every change, which unreviewed LLM output makes unsolvable ([episode](../sources/creator-of-c-bell-labs-negative-overhead.md)); a machine-checked proof attached to each change is the only answer anyone in this corpus has proposed to that. [Marc Brooker](../entities/marc-brooker.md)'s prediction that the mainstream moves to specification-driven agentic development ([episode](../sources/aws-distinguished-eng-learnings-from.md)) is the same forecast without the verification layer, from the same employer. And the tension with the AI-tools optimists ([ai-coding-tools](ai-coding-tools.md)) remains real: where Bolin and Novati celebrate 80–90% model-written code, Leroy answers that checking, not writing, is the binding cost — and de Moura's answer to Leroy is that the checking can now be mechanized too.

### Contradiction filed 2026-08-17: is a proof enough to stop understanding the code?

[Anders Hejlsberg](../entities/anders-hejlsberg.md) (2026-08-17) does not discuss formal verification, but his answer to the same underlying question is the strongest objection in the corpus to de Moura's, and it comes from outside the field entirely ([episode](../sources/creator-of-typescript-10x-faster.md)). Asked whether IDEs and human understanding of code become unnecessary, he refuses on accountability grounds: someone has to take responsibility when a system causes harm, and without understanding the code you cannot fix the bug or respond to a security breach. "Good luck to you."

The objection bites at a specific seam in de Moura's argument. A machine-checked proof discharges *correctness relative to a specification*; it does not discharge the other things a maintainer is accountable for — modifying the system, diagnosing a failure whose cause lies outside the specification, or explaining the system to a regulator or a court. De Moura would likely answer that the specification is exactly where that attention should go, which is his stated position; the unresolved part is whether the median engineer is ever in a position to formalize enough of the system for that trade to be available. Note the corpus asymmetry: de Moura's is a report from a verified-codebase world (Lean, [AWS](../entities/aws.md) accelerator compilers), while Hejlsberg's is a report from the largest unverified codebase any guest maintains.

### Contradiction filed 2026-08-10: does AI make readability matter more, or not at all?

[Roberto Ierusalimschy](../entities/roberto-ierusalimschy.md) (2026-08-03) argued that because the human's remaining job under AI is *reading and verifying*, simplicity becomes more valuable, not less ([episode](../sources/creator-of-lua-scripting-programming.md)). De Moura (2026-08-10) reaches the opposite conclusion from the same premise about who writes the code: if a machine-checked proof carries the correctness claim, nobody needs to read the implementation. "In the past, people say, 'Oh, functional programming, I don't like it.' But if I'm not the one that's writing most of the code anyway, it doesn't really matter. What matters? The specification level. It doesn't really matter how the code has been written" ([episode](../sources/creator-of-lean-the-end-of-handwritten.md)). The disagreement is really about where human attention relocates — to the *code* (Ierusalimschy) or to the *specification* (de Moura) — and it is unresolved in the corpus. Note the practical asymmetry: de Moura's position requires a proof assistant in the loop, so it is a claim about verified codebases, not about the median repository.

## Practical takeaways

- Treat crashing code as a security hole, not a quality nit — "never crashes" is a meaningful, checkable spec ([Leroy](../sources/creator-of-ocaml-functional-programming.md)).
- Reason with invariants, not execution traces; behaviors explode combinatorially ([Lamport](../sources/turing-award-winner-on-working-with.md)). With dependent types you can make the invariant a field's *type* and stop inventing them by hand ([de Moura](../sources/creator-of-lean-the-end-of-handwritten.md)).
- Write your slow, obviously-correct version first and treat it as the specification; ask for the optimized version plus a proof of equivalence ([de Moura](../sources/creator-of-lean-the-end-of-handwritten.md)).
- Formalize when the thing is safety-critical **or when you don't really understand it** — formalizing is the strongest comprehension tool available, and it is what makes aggressive optimization safe ([de Moura](../sources/creator-of-lean-the-end-of-handwritten.md)).
- Write code you must reason about in as pure/functional a style as possible — it shrinks the program-to-math gap ([Leroy](../sources/creator-of-ocaml-functional-programming.md)).
- Undecidability is not an excuse: you only need proofs for the programs you care about ([Leroy](../sources/creator-of-ocaml-functional-programming.md)), and real programs are correct for simple reasons, which is why bounded solvers work ([de Moura](../sources/creator-of-lean-the-end-of-handwritten.md)).
- If you accept AI-generated code, push for machine-checkable evidence rather than human review of bulk output — and scrutinize the *specification*, including whether preconditions are satisfiable ([Leroy](../sources/creator-of-ocaml-functional-programming.md)). The counterweight is that proof upkeep, historically the reason teams abandoned verification, is now the part AI does best ([de Moura](../sources/creator-of-lean-the-end-of-handwritten.md)).
- To learn a proof assistant in 2026, put an agent in a third pane next to your code and the Infoview and tell it what you already know ([de Moura](../sources/creator-of-lean-the-end-of-handwritten.md)).
