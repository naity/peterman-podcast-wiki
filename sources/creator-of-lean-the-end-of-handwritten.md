# Creator of Lean: Handwritten Math Will Change Dramatically | Leonardo de Moura

Leonardo de Moura built Z3, the SMT solver that has quietly sat under a generation of program-analysis tools, and then built [Lean](../concepts/formal-verification.md) to fix what Z3 could not do — prove the *absence* of bugs rather than find their presence. Ryan Peterman opens with the same [Dijkstra](../entities/edsger-dijkstra.md) line [Xavier Leroy](../entities/xavier-leroy.md) used three weeks earlier ("program testing can be used to show the presence of bugs, but never to show their absence"), and de Moura answers it with a claim the corpus has not heard before: the thing that made verification unaffordable was never writing the specification, it was writing and *maintaining* the proofs — and AI has just eaten that cost. The episode is the practitioner's report from the other side of Leroy's "maybe this will be the decade of formal verification" bet, including a colleague who got an AI to translate zlib from C into Lean and prove that decompression inverts compression, in one week.

## Key takeaways

- **Lean is a programming language that also lets you state and prove properties about your programs, with machine-checkable proofs.** Two routes exist for code that isn't written in Lean: *shallow embedding* (the Aeneas tool maps Rust into Lean and you verify the translation) and *deep embedding* (you write the semantics of C inside Lean, so C programs become Lean objects you can state theorems about). His compressed version of the whole discipline: "if you can express in math what you care about in your program, you can verify using Lean" ([formal-verification](../concepts/formal-verification.md)).
- **Only the kernel has to be trusted, and it is small enough to rewrite.** Proof checking in Lean *is* type checking: you export a development as a blob and a checker verifies that each term's type matches the type it claims. A high-performance kernel is about 5,000 lines — "the goal of the kernel should be something you can write yourself" — and the shipped kernel is deliberately not the only one. Mario Carneiro's "Lean for Lean" kernel is written in Lean and verified against Lean's semantics; others exist in Rust and elsewhere, plus pretty-printers so you can confirm the theorem exported really was Fermat's Last Theorem and not 2 + 3 = 4. Lean itself is *not* verified, and he explains why it can't be: nobody can write a stable specification for something like the simplifier, because users keep asking to change its behaviour.
- **The economics of verification just inverted, and the spec was never the bottleneck.** Historically verification cost roughly 10x the program, and the recurring pain was that every code change broke proofs you then had to repair, often without remembering why they were written — "imagine with proofs, it's the same process" as patching a test suite, but worse. AI is "extremely good at proving, writing formal proofs, maintaining formal proofs"; he describes asking it, the day before recording, to rewrite proofs he did not himself understand so he could remove a feature. On specifications he is much less worried than the skeptics: "properties are usually vaguely in your mind," and — the line worth stealing — "an inefficient program can be viewed as a specification." Write the naive version, then ask the AI for the fast one plus a proof of equivalence ([ai-era-engineering](../concepts/ai-era-engineering.md)).
- **The zlib demonstration.** Kim Morrison prompted an AI to translate the zlib C compression library into Lean, pass zlib's own test suites, and then prove that compressing and decompressing returns the original data. It worked in about a week, and the ongoing phase is asking for optimizations under the constraint that the proofs must still go through. "Six months ago, I would say this is science fiction." This is the concrete counterexample to the assumption that a spec-plus-proof pipeline is a decade away.
- **Proofs buy optimization courage, and that is his headline argument for adopting them.** He says people routinely refuse to optimize code because they don't understand why it currently works; a proof removes that fear, and formalizing anything leaves you understanding it far better than merely implementing it did. Applied to agents: "if the AI is telling you, look, I optimized it, it still computes the same thing. Here's the proof" — versus today's workflow of manually inspecting an agent's diff for introduced bugs. His own practice has already moved: "I'm not writing unit tests anymore. I'm writing properties and proving them. The AI is proving most of them."
- **Handwritten math changes "dramatically" but does not end.** The frontier labs only recently started training seriously on Lean with real RL pipelines — before that it was "in the data sets" — so he expects costs to keep falling and Lean/Rocq to go mainstream. Handwritten proofs survive as *communication artifacts*, the way handmade furniture survives machine production: "there will always be people polishing, making them super easy to understand for another human." And humans stay structurally in the loop because specifications are the interface between AI-generated math and anything we actually want: without it "this is some alien thing that's going by itself."
- **A dissent from the readability camp, one week after [Roberto Ierusalimschy](../entities/roberto-ierusalimschy.md) made the opposite argument.** De Moura thinks proofs make code *aesthetics* irrelevant rather than more important: "in the past, people say, 'Oh, functional programming, I don't like it.' But if I'm not the one that's writing most of the code anyway, it doesn't really matter. What matters? The specification level. It doesn't really matter how the code has been written" ([functional-programming](../concepts/functional-programming.md)). Where Ierusalimschy says the human's remaining job is reading, de Moura says the human's remaining job is *specifying* and the machine does the reading.
- **AI finds proofs; it does not yet invent mathematics.** "We see no evidence it can find novel proofs" of the concept-inventing kind — the million-line refutation of the unit distance conjecture is a formal proof, not a new theory, and "they did not come up with a new theory or anything like that." Lean Eval, his team's challenge site, now includes problems where the AI must construct the mathematical objects itself. His hedge is explicit: "I would not bet against AI here."
- **How Lean won the mathematicians, and why that mattered more than verification.** mathlib started in 2017; the Liquid Tensor Experiment (2020) formalized an unpublished result Fields Medalist Peter Scholze was himself unsure of. The startling part is that Johan Commelin's team formalized *and simplified* the proof without fully understanding it, guided step by step by the Infoview — "how can you simplify a proof from one of the greatest living mathematicians without fully understanding it?" The lesson he draws is social rather than technical: formalization is "not about verifying, but enabling people to work together in large numbers," because you no longer need to trust a stranger's proof to let them fill a hole in yours. Terence Tao arrived afterwards and "got addicted."
- **Proof as a single-player game — which is exactly why AI can play it.** Users tell him "you built my favorite computer game"; he compares it to "solving millions of really hard Sudoku problems." The AI plays the identical game: take a statement, open a `by` block, apply tactics while watching the Infoview state change, and win when there are no goals left — with RL optimizing toward that terminal state. DeepMind's silver medal in 2024 shocked him; there are now golds from Harmonic and from ByteDance, which he was surprised to learn runs a 400-person math team.
- **Z3 vs Lean is automatic vs interactive, and Lean exists because automatic ran out of steam.** He started Z3 twenty years ago at [Microsoft](../entities/microsoft.md) Research: a push-button SMT solver with background theories (arithmetic, arrays) chosen for test-case generation and software verification. It excels at *finding* bugs — encode a suspicious path as constraints, get "unsatisfiable" (impossible to reach) or a concrete triggering input — and at bounded hardware verification. It was "never super successful" at proving absence of bugs, because the heuristics for quantified properties were hand-coded and failed, leaving users saying "come on, I know the proof, why can't Z3 find it?" — "that's why Lean started." A colleague at [Amazon](../entities/amazon.md) named the failure mode *proof instability*: write `B and A` instead of `A and B` and the proof flips to failing. In Lean you control the proof step by step, which is what lets a human — or now an AI — supply the reasoning the solver couldn't guess ([complexity-theory](../concepts/complexity-theory.md)).
- **Why undecidable tools work anyway.** Z3 spans the whole ladder (NP-complete, PSPACE, EXPTIME, undecidable), and tiny SAT instances exist that no solver cracks — yet bounded real-world problems yield easily, and his explanation is a good one: "programs and hardware, they are not correct for esoteric reasons... they are correct for very simple reasons."
- **Lean was an order of magnitude harder than Z3, and the reason was the humans.** Z3's interface is SMT-LIB, a machine-facing language, and its users are "very sophisticated software developers, people that speak the same language I speak." Lean has libraries, interactivity, an LSP, a build system, GitHub, and a first user base of *mathematicians* with wholly different expectations — plus a second constituency wanting a general-purpose language, when "I'm not a programming language person. My background is automated reasoning." The single hardest artifact was the C++ → Lean self-hosting rewrite for Lean 4: ~100,000 lines, all human-written, bootstrapped with deliberately bare-bones features, failing file by file down a 1,000-file pipeline, with the required proofs constructed with no interactivity at all — "almost like programming in assembly." "I literally wanted to cry when I managed to compile Lean 4." Many people expected him and Sebastian Ullrich to fail.
- **What users actually cite as Lean's edge: extensibility, mathlib, community, and responsiveness.** Because Lean is implemented in Lean, you can write automation mid-proof — and AIs now do it unprompted, writing Lean metaprograms to test their own conjectures about why something fails. Two outside artifacts illustrate the reach: Patrick Massot, a French mathematician with no CS degree, built Lean Verbose, which renders proofs as structured English (or French) textbook prose with clickable next-move suggestions; and Veil, a DSL for protocol verification that feels like a separate system but is just a Lean file. Both were built without ever contacting the core team ([developer-tools](../concepts/developer-tools.md)). mathlib matters because you need the concepts to even *state* a conjecture. And the community: pre-AI, Zulip answers arrived in five minutes — "people would say human beats AI" — while Jeremy Avigad, the first user, could request a feature and get it the same day ([open-source](../concepts/open-source.md)).
- **Dependent type theory was a user-driven choice, not an aesthetic one.** Higher-order logic is far easier to implement and was his instinct; Avigad convinced him he would never attract Fields-Medal-level mathematicians without dependent types, because abstract math needs structures as first-class citizens — a function that takes a group and returns a group — where higher-order logic forces "encoding tricks. It's a mess." He reports unanimity among the serious users (Tao, Alex Kontorovich, Avigad, Kevin Buzzard, Massot). The payoff is invariants for free: a struct field whose *type* is a proof that x > y, or a function you cannot call without supplying evidence that y ≠ 0 — "you don't have to invent invariants." The historic objection was that supplying those proofs is annoying, and that is precisely what AI removes ([programming-languages](../concepts/programming-languages.md)).
- **Thirteen years, ten of them as a research project.** Lean became "a product" only when he and Sebastian Ullrich founded the nonprofit in 2023 and could hire engineers — funded on the strength of Lean's impact in math, with [AWS](../entities/aws.md)/Amazon as the largest donors so far ([academia-vs-industry](../concepts/academia-vs-industry.md)). The direction the money is pushing is the underfunded half: Lean as a general programming language for software and hardware verification. At AWS, formal verification has been used "for decades, but only for the super-safe, critical components because it's expensive" — and there is now a half-million-line Lean compiler for AI accelerators where proofs are the bonus rather than the point.
- **Math and software verification scale in opposite directions.** In math the statements are tiny (Fermat's Last Theorem) and the proofs are deep; in software the statements are big and the proofs shallow but forced to manipulate enormous objects. Scalability, not expressiveness, is the open problem.
- **How to learn it now: talk to an agent.** The books are at lean-lang.org (Functional Programming in Lean, Theorem Proving in Lean, Mathematics in Lean, *The Mechanics of Proof*), but his actual recommendation is a three-pane setup — code, Infoview, and an AI agent writing and narrating. Terence Tao learned Lean pre-agents by copy-pasting between ChatGPT and VS Code. Tell the agent your background ("if you tell, oh, I know Haskell, it's so much easier") and it adapts ([teaching-and-communication](../concepts/teaching-and-communication.md)).
- **Advice to his younger self: work on people skills.** "I'm super introverted. And I would tell, look, you should work on your people skills because it helps a lot... when you have to interact with a community, with people. For me, it was hard to learn that." He also declines to warn himself about the difficulty — "eagerness is bliss. I mean, you don't know how hard things are, and when you start the adventure" ([regrets-and-advice](../concepts/regrets-and-advice.md)).
