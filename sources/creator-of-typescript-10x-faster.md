# Creator of TypeScript: 10x Faster Typescript, Why AI Won't Replace SWEs | Anders Hejlsberg

Anders Hejlsberg has shipped four widely-used languages across four decades — Turbo Pascal at Borland (written entirely in Z80 assembly), Delphi, C# at [Microsoft](../entities/microsoft.md), and [TypeScript](../concepts/programming-languages.md), which passed JavaScript and Python to become the most-used language on GitHub last year. The occasion for the episode is the native rewrite: the TypeScript compiler, famously written in JavaScript and self-hosted, has been ported to Go. Ryan Peterman uses that decision as the door into two larger arguments — why *self-hosting inside the ecosystem you want to join* beats targeting it from outside, and why Hejlsberg, unlike the frontier labs, thinks compilers, IDEs and junior engineers all survive the AI transition. The episode is unusual in the corpus for being told by someone who is still writing the compiler himself at the top of the [Microsoft](../entities/microsoft.md) ladder, and it lands directly on top of the [Ierusalimschy](../entities/roberto-ierusalimschy.md)/[de Moura](../entities/leonardo-de-moura.md) argument about what human attention is for under AI.

## Key takeaways

- **Writing the compiler in JavaScript was a distribution decision, not a performance one.** Early prototypes were in C, adapting an Internet Explorer JavaScript parser; moving to JavaScript bought self-hosting (the team became daily users of their own product) and ubiquity (the compiler ran in the browser, which native code could not before WebAssembly). "If you can self-host in the ecosystem that you want to be a part of, that is dramatically better than targeting from outside." V8's optimization work had brought JavaScript to within 2–3x of native, which made the choice affordable.
- **The rewrite was forced by concurrency, not by the 2–3x.** JavaScript is single-threaded and web workers cannot share data structures, so the compiler could not use multiple cores: "Moore's Law has stopped giving us faster CPUs, it's giving us more CPUs." Scale did the rest — VS Code is ~2.3 million lines of code, a magnitude nobody anticipated when TypeScript started in 2012, and TypeScript 6 runs at only about 50% the speed of TypeScript 1.5 despite better algorithms, because projects grew and checking got more sophisticated.
- **Why Go and not Rust.** They chose to *port* rather than rewrite, to preserve semantics and backward compatibility — and the existing codebase assumes garbage collection and first-class functions. Rust has no GC, and its borrow checker cannot express the circular structures that permeate a compiler (parent pointers, recursive types, symbol references). "We tried, and it just wasn't feasible." Go supplied native codegen across platforms, GC, and shared-memory concurrency.
- **The migration pattern he recommends is AI-writes-the-tool, not AI-does-the-migration.** They hand-wrote a syntactic translator from TypeScript to Go that produced compiling-adjacent code needing type-system refactoring. Pointing a model at half a million to a million lines leaves you verifying all of it against hallucination; better to have AI generate a *deterministic* tool, which shrinks the validation surface to the tool. His homely version: asking an AI to settle trip expenses failed, asking it to write the spreadsheet program worked ([ai-coding-tools](../concepts/ai-coding-tools.md)).
- **AI entrenches incumbent languages.** Models are best at whatever they saw most in training, and teaching a niche language costs prompt tokens — so the bar for a new language, already high (compiler, tooling, language services, debugger, profiler, frameworks, multiple targets), rises further. He also rejects the idea of LLMs skipping languages and emitting machine code: source text is the most compressed form, closest to meaning, while machine code is full of noise (addresses, register choices) irrelevant to the logic.
- **Fast tooling matters *more* under AI, which is the opposite of the usual assumption.** Agents write code in the background and make human-like mistakes at multiplied volume; "if type checking takes two minutes every time the LLM writes something, that's horrible." And agents increasingly need *semantic* operations — renaming a property called `version` cannot be done by grep without hitting unrelated interfaces — so "the only thing that can do semantic search is a compiler," reached through LSP or a CLI ([developer-tools](../concepts/developer-tools.md)).
- **His pushback on the AI-writes-everything claims is a quality claim, not a volume claim.** AI already writes 100% of some categories of application, and the 90% statistic is partly self-fulfilling because AI's sheer volume skews it — but "high quality code that no one has ever written before, it's not clear AI will write 90% of that." His example of code models cannot write well is the TypeScript compiler itself: novel algorithms outside the training distribution.
- **On IDEs going away: "good luck to you."** The argument is accountability rather than capability — someone has to take responsibility when an AI-built system causes harm, and without understanding the code you cannot fix a bug or respond to a breach. On juniors: "if it does, then how do we ever get senior software engineers?" He expects the pyramid to narrow at the base and the job to shift from typing to reviewing agent output — the craft changes rather than disappears ([ai-era-engineering](../concepts/ai-era-engineering.md)).
- **He stayed an IC deliberately, after finding out he hated the alternative.** He worked alone on Turbo Pascal, learned to delegate as machines outgrew one person, then spent the C# years mostly on design while others coded — a prestigious role in which he drifted into being an "architecture astronaut" and felt unfulfilled. TypeScript put him back in the compiler. His general advice is to fit the role to the person rather than the trajectory, and his argument for architects staying in some corner of the code is credibility: you can't propose a real refactoring you don't understand ([regrets-and-advice](../concepts/regrets-and-advice.md)).
- **Two constraints, forty years apart, as the hardest work he's done.** Recently: shared-memory concurrency in the Go port — many CPUs over shared structures without anyone clobbering anyone. Originally: Turbo Pascal's compiler, editor and runtime all in Z80 assembly on a 64k machine, counting single bytes ("I'm 20 bytes over budget here") by turning a long jump into a short jump to an adjacent jump. He compares it to woodworking, and notes the constraint has been replaced by effectively bottomless capacity ([computer-architecture](../concepts/computer-architecture.md)).
- **Book: *Algorithms + Data Structures = Programs* by Niklaus Wirth** — light on math, heavy on instructive examples. He learned hash tables from it; Turbo Pascal's symbol tables had been linked lists, and switching made the compiler twice as fast. Out of print 30 years, free as a PDF.
- **Advice to his younger self:** learn teamwork earlier (his solo-perfectionist habits had to be deliberately unlearned), and don't accept other people's impossibility claims — when people said Turbo Pascal couldn't be built, that described the limits of their understanding, not reality.
